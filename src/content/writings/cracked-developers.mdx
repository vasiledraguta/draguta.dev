## 1. What I consider a cracked developer

### Where I come from

I grew up in the Republic of Moldova, a country most people will struggle to point to on a map. I did not meet a single person with a gravitational pull towards building cool things until my first year of university. It makes sense. In a poor former Soviet republic people are busy closing the bottom of Maslow's pyramid. Nobody has the room to think about the top of it.

Since then I have been trying to work out what those people run on. I think it is two things.

### High agency

A term I took from [George Mack](https://www.highagency.com). Most people treat the future as fixed. Some treat it as something you can push on, and those are the high agency ones. Mack splits it into three parts that rarely show up together: _clear thinking_, a _bias to action_, and _disagreeability_. Without clear thinking you run at the first bad idea in your head. Without action the idea stays a thought. Without disagreeability you stop the moment someone tells you no.

### Prolific

You make a lot of things and most of them are bad. [Dean Keith Simonton](https://jamesclear.com/equal-odds) counted the output of scientists, composers and writers and found _your number of hits is roughly proportional to your number of attempts_. Paul Erdős wrote over 1,500 papers and most were never cited. Those forgotten papers are the reason the famous ones exist. Skills do improve with practice, so the rule is not exact, but the conclusion holds: make more things.

_Everything below is my reading of my own data. Take it as an opinion._

## 2. Why should you care

Will becoming a cracked dev make you happy? Honestly, no idea. It is not for everybody, and you should ask yourself what you actually want before you copy anyone.

### Agency guarantees nothing

In 1990 a team left Apple to build a handheld device that was a phone and a computer at the same time. The company doing that called General Magic went bankrupt. A junior engineer there called Tony Fadell later built the iPhone.

### The people I cannot see

I cannot show you the people who had all of this and got nothing. They never got famous enough for me to find, which is the whole problem. I only looked at people who already made it, so I have no idea what share of high agency developers fail. All I can tell you is that I did not find one who made it without it. You don't know where you end up until you try.

## 3. What the median numbers tell us

### The dataset

391 developers from one GitHub snapshot, each with at least one repo over 250 stars. I use medians here, because a few huge accounts drag the averages around: average followers 2,815, median 1,116.

### Most of them are not famous

187 of the 391 have fewer than a thousand followers, and the lowest has 157. For the median developer, the best repo has about 2.7 times more stars than they have followers. People found the project first and the person second.

### It took a decade

The median account was created in 2015. Only 49 joined in 2020 or later. And 354 of them still pushed code in 2026.

### Most of what they built, nobody saw

The median developer has 38 public repos. The best one sits at 3,904 stars, the second at 770, the fifth at 103, and for half of them the fifth is under 100. That is the equal odds rule sitting in a dataset. You see one repo. You do not see the other 37, and the other 37 are why the one exists.

## 4. What I learned

### Maintaining beats launching

I expected the biggest group to be people with one viral project, but it is not. 130 of the 391 got there by keeping the same tool alive for years, against 93 who did it with a single repo. Boring, and it works.

### Stars and followers are different games

The one-hit people have about ten times the stars of the maintainers and roughly the same number of followers. The only group with real followings built an audience on purpose, and their repos are smaller. Pick which game you are playing.

### Nobody arrived

Most of them are still pushing code, years after the thing that made them known.

### The one line

If you take one line from this: **the only number in this dataset you control is how many times you try.**
